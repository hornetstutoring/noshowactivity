1. Targeted Messages to Students[back to top of page]


This lesson includes:

  • 1. Identifying students that you can send a personalized message to
  • 2. Reviewing some template messages
  • 3. Writing personalized messages to three to five students
  • 4. Reflection

Sending a personalized message to students in the class can be an effective way to encourage them to attend group study sessions and to make them feel cared for.

Identifying students that you can send a personalized message to

It can be helpful to look at a list of students while you think. I recommend that you open the canvas page and look at the list of students’ names while you consider each of these questions. Hopefully at the end, you will have three to five students that you have chosen to receive a personalized message.

  • Are there any students that you have interacted with recently during class? When you make a connection with a student in class, that does not always lead to them attending study sessions, but a quick follow up message can make them feel seen and included.
  • What about students that have missed a class recently? Letting someone know that you noticed their absence can be a friendly way to show them that you care. If you cannot think of a name, your professor may be helpful with this. Ask your professor for the names of some students that have unexpectedly missed classes recently.
  • Can you identify students who attended study sessions in the past, but who have not returned in a while? Sometimes students get busy and start to prioritize other things. This is understandable, but a little personalized message may help them to make sure that they are giving the class their full attention.
  • Can you think of a student who made an interesting contribution during class? For example, is there a student who asked an interesting question or contributed to group work? When you let a student know that you notice their cool behavior in class, it can lead to a sparking a connection with them. People like to be noticed like that.
  • Can you think of a student that you have not connected with yet, but that you can picture in your head? If there is something positive and inoffensive about this person that you can respectfully comment on, that might be a good way to try to spark a connection with them. For example, “I noticed you in class the other day and you were wearing a tee-shirt of my favorite band. My favorite song of theirs is X. How about you?” Or “We have not been able to connect in class, but I wanted to tell you that I notice the way you take notes with all the different colored pencils. That seems cool, and I would love to learn more about your system!”

Once you have identified a handful of students, move onto the next part.

Reviewing some template messages

Keep the message short and sweet, but always include the relevant information about your next group study session, including time/location/topic. Also, be sure to include their name in the initial greeting (e.g. “Hi Dimitrius!”). Here are some sample templates:

I really enjoyed our conversation this week about multiple regressions. I was thinking about what I explained to you, and I hope that it was clear. Please message me if you have any follow up questions. I also wanted to let you know that I will be covering this topic in the group study session on Wednesday @ 1-1:50 in the ASC. We will be practicing some problems, and I will be able to help you really master that content. I hope to see you then!

I noticed that you were not in class this week. I hope that everything is okay. I missed your funny comments during group work. I look forward to seeing you in class very soon. Please let me know if I can be any help.

I was just thinking about the awesome job that you did in class when you were sharing the research that your group found about prisons. Really impressive! I hope to see you in my group study sessions this week. We will be planning our outlines for the in-class essay. I would love to have a chance to see what cool ideas you have about the topic!

Writing personalized messages to three to five students

Make sure that the message is written in your voice and reflects your personality. Try to include something in the message that only applies to this particular student. That is what makes it a personal message.

Also, be careful that you are not being overly intimate or crossing anyone’s boundaries. You don’t want to accidentally come off as a creep.

Once you have finished sending your messages move on to the reflection.

Reflection

We will complete this task in a separate window, so first copy one of the messages that you sent.

Then, prepare a response to tell us about the message and the student. Reflect on how you can continue to use personalized messages to connect with students in the class.

2. Engaging with Students During Class Time [back to top of page]


This lesson includes:

  • 1. Initial reflection
  • 2. Review of the guidebook policy related to how to act during class time
  • 3. Plan a strategy
  • 4. Submit a final reflection

The reason that we place tutors in the class is so that they can connect with students and support them during class. Connecting with students during class time can be challenging, but it is critical to improving attendance during group study sessions.

Initial reflection

How effective is your time during class? What are the obstacles that keep you from connecting with students? Do any of these factors play a role?

  • The professor lectures the whole time, so I don’t have many opportunities to talk with students.
  • I am shy and/or intimidated by the students in the class.
  • I don’t know what to say.
  • I do not have a clear role in the classroom most days. I wish I had more direction.

Once you have identified a few obstacles, continue to the next section.

Review of the guidebook policy related to how to act during class time

This is from the guidebook:

Embedded Tutors generally attend every class meeting for at least one hour. In high-unit classes (such as those with a lab), tutors may attend a portion of the class time. This will be worked out in advance with staff. We want tutors working during class time to help students. The exact nature of the help that tutors can provide will vary depending on the course, the instructor, and the modality of the class.

Tutors should work with instructors to determine the best times to attend the class. This is especially important for high-unit classes or classes with a lecture and a lab. Tutors should use class time to help students. Here are some core concepts to get started:

  • If a tutor does not have any other way to contribute to student learning, the default position should be to model strong study skills: taking notes, occasionally asking questions, staying off cell phones, etc.
  • Use class time to establish rapport with students. Introduce yourself to the class in the first week of the semester and participate in any activities.
  • The instructor should set aside a minute or two at the start of each class—or at another regular and convenient time—for the tutor to announce the time, location, and topic for the upcoming tutoring session
  • Some tutors find that passing around a sign-up sheet during class can help with attendance in study sessions. The sign-up sheet is not a binding commitment, but sometimes writing their name down can be a good first step to getting them to the sessions. Also, when students sign up, you can follow up later with an email.
  • Tutors generally find that it is much easier to help students during class when the students are engaged in group work or problem-based learning, such as workshop, labs, discussion groups, etc. Tutors should work with the instructor to determine the best way to help students. Here are some concepts to consider:
  • Tutors can check in with each group for roughly the same amount of time. This is intuitive but may not be the best way to do it.
  • The instructor may be able to help identify a group that would benefit from some extra direction, and the tutors can join their group for an extended period of time.
  • Or, when group work begins, the tutor can wait a couple minutes and scan the room to see which groups might benefit from some extra direction. Do some groups look confused, distracted, or slow to start?
  • Be thoughtful about how to approach students and engage with them. It is generally not best to go to each group and say, “How is it going?” because a lot of people will just say “fine” regardless. Instead, try something like: “What did your group get for question three? That was a hard one.”
  • Be gently intrusive. We do not subscribe to a “come see me for help” model. We want to take the help to the students. Try to get eyes on the students’ work to check their understanding of the concepts. When students are on the wrong track or struggling to get started, help them with clues or redirection. Take the initiative to help them but also be careful not to smother them. When a student sends you the vibe that they want to be left alone, then you should back off.
  • Regularly, talk with the instructor about how you should engage with students in class. The faculty are the subject experts, and tutors should always respect that the faculty have authority over their classroom. If you have ideas about howe to engage with students, you should talk to the instructor ahead of time to make sure they support your ideas.

Always remember to act in a manner that is professional, inclusive, and student-centered:

  • Do not study for your other classes.
  • Arrive and depart at the agreed upon time; do not leave unexpectedly unless it is an unavoidable emergency.
  • Alert the instructor, students, and HT staff whenever you are absent.
  • Do not challenge or contradict the instructor.
  • Change seats every day, even if students do not want to give up their seats. (This allows you to meet more students).
  • Sit with students, never in front of the room.
  • Do not be disruptive.
  • Do not use your phone during class.
  • Always use inclusive language.
  • “Move toward” students who are marginalized or seem to be struggling in class.
Plan a strategy

As you read through the text in the guidebook, were you inspired with any ideas?

How you act in the class could impact what the professor has planned. Also, you might need the professor’s support for your plan. Consider how you might initiate a conversation with the professor about how to better help students during class. Make sure that your approach could not be interpreted as an attack on the professor in any way. Consider sending an email message with something like this:

I have been feeling like I am missing opportunities to help students during class. I have a couple ideas, but I wanted to talk with you about them first. Can we have a short conversation about this when I see you on Monday

Think about #10 above. Can you think of a couple individual students in the class that might benefit from you “moving toward” them? Could one of these ideas work for you?

  • 1. Sit near the student during class
  • 2. Catch the student before class or during the break
  • 3. Plan a conversation starter ahead of time

If the professor is primarily lecturing, what are some subtle ways to help nearby students without disrupting class. Maybe ask questions on behalf of nearby students. Maybe check in with students that seem lost by writing a note on the edge of your paper and showing it to them discreetly. It is a good idea to talk about these kinds of strategies with your professor, so that they do not get the impression that you are being disruptive.

If you are looking for better ways to engage with the class, you can also bring the conversation up in your mentor meetings.
